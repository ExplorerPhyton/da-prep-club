Writing a personal statement that doesn't sound like everyone else's

ESSAYS · 6 MIN READ

Every reader on a scholarship committee has read the same four essays a hundred times: the sports injury that taught resilience, the immigrant-family sacrifice, the volunteer trip that "opened my eyes," and the grandparent who inspired a career choice. None of those experiences are wrong to write about. What sinks them is that the writer answers the prompt the way they think it should be answered, instead of the way they'd actually explain it to a friend.

Start with a moment, not a theme

Committees skim. An essay that opens with a general statement about your values reads like every other essay in the stack. An essay that opens mid-scene — a specific sentence someone said, a specific object in your hand, a specific decision you made in under ten seconds — reads like it belongs to one person. Pick the smallest possible moment that contains the whole story, and start there.

Say what you actually think, not what sounds good

A common failure mode is writing the "safe" version of an opinion because it seems like what a scholarship committee wants to hear. Readers can tell. The essays that stand out usually have one sentence that a cautious writer would have cut — a real doubt, a real disagreement, a real mistake. That sentence is often the reason the essay gets remembered.

Cut the summary paragraph

Most drafts end with a paragraph that restates everything the essay just showed: "This experience taught me the value of hard work and perseverance." If the essay did its job, the reader already knows that. Cutting the summary and ending on the last concrete detail almost always makes the essay stronger.

Get a second reader before you submit

Not for grammar — for the question "what does this essay actually tell you about me that you didn't already know?" If a reader can't answer that in one sentence, there's still a revision to do. The peer review sign-up in the resource library exists for exactly this.
